In the open-source community, one of the most rewarding challenges is turning passionate users into active contributors — and eventually, project stakeholders. At ORAS, we've seen this transition form a virtuous cycle: **adopter → contributor → integrator**. This feedback loop is not only key to our project’s growth, but also a sustainable model for community development.

In this post, I’ll share a strategy that has worked well in a CNCF Project ORAS. I am a maintainer of the ORAS Project. The goal is simple: **empower users to take ownership of the very ideas they bring to the table**, and grow with the project as it evolves.

## 👀 Step 1: Monitor Community Activity

Every contributor starts as a user. My first step is to closely monitor incoming questions, feature requests, and bug reports — whether they come through **GitHub Issues** or our **Slack workspace**.

When a user not only submits an issue but is also willing to dive deep, clarify, and iterate, that’s usually a sign they’re professionally invested. Maybe they’re trying to solve a real problem in their team. This level of engagement is the seed of potential contribution.

> 🔍 **Tip:** Don’t just respond with answers. Ask questions, dig deeper, and make them feel heard.

## 👤 Step 2: Assess Contributor Potential

Once I spot a potential contributor, I do a quick background check — not for gatekeeping, but for understanding.

I’ll look at:

* Their **GitHub profile**: Are they active in other OSS projects?
* Their **organization**: Do they work at a tech-forward company where OSS contribution is encouraged?

This helps me tailor the outreach. If they’re already contributing elsewhere, odds are they know the workflows and can ramp up quickly.


## 🤝 Step 3: Build a Relationship, Not Just a PR

Next, I reach out directly — often on **Slack**, **LinkedIn**, or **X (formerly Twitter)** — to introduce myself and start a conversation.

I’ll ask about:

* Their **use case**
* How they discovered ORAS
* Whether they’re using it in production

This isn’t just about getting a code contribution — it’s about **building trust**. People are more likely to contribute when they know someone from the project team personally cares about their goals.

> 💡 If someone has already integrated your tool into a product or system, they’re practically halfway to being a contributor — they just might not know it yet.


## ✉️ Step 4: Invite Them to Contribute (Publicly)

Once there's mutual understanding, I extend a public, open invitation:
*"Would you be interested in contributing this feature or fix yourself?"*

Surprisingly, most users respond with honesty. Some say they’re swamped, while others jump at the chance. Even when the answer is "not now", the relationship is already built — and often they return when the timing is better.

## 🏁 The Result: A Contributor Flywheel

This approach turns support interactions into a growth engine. We’ve seen users:

* Start by opening an issue
* Contribute a small fix
* Join community calls
* Eventually become maintainers or integrators

It’s not about volume; it’s about consistency and care. **One engaged contributor is worth more than ten casual users**.

## 🎯 Bonus Insight: Engage End-User Companies Differently

Getting large end-user companies like **Mercedes-Benz** or **GE HealthCare** to contribute code is understandably harder. But that doesn’t mean they can’t play a role in community growth.

These companies are often happy to:

* Share their success stories at **KubeCon** or other industry events
* Co-author **case studies or blog posts**
* Provide validation and credibility for your OSS project

Promoting these use cases publicly helps grow awareness and adoption — and that’s equally important for a healthy community.

## Final Thoughts

Contributor growth doesn’t happen by chance. It happens when **you meet users where they are**, build trust, and create low-friction paths to participation.

At ORAS, this contributor engagement strategy has become one of our most valuable tools for sustainability. As more projects look to scale, I hope this story helps others adopt similar human-centered, relationship-driven approaches.
